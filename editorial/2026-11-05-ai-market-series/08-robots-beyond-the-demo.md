# Look past the humanoid demo: where robots are being bought

Status: unpublished research brief; editorial review pending. Research cutoff: November 5, 2026. Proposed format: 1,200–1,500-word manufacturing and logistics feature. Audience: operations staff, technicians, warehouse workers, supply-chain managers, and career changers.

## The angle

Move the conversation from viral demonstrations to measured commercial adoption. Compare the scale and purpose of established industrial and logistics robotics with the newer humanoid market, then identify the extra evidence needed to discuss labor consequences.

## Proposed opening

A robot does not need to look like a person to change a workplace. A machine moving materials through a warehouse may tell us more about commercial automation than a spectacular demonstration. The useful questions are what it does repeatedly, how often it works, and how people work around it.

## Evidence to build on

- [IFR's September 24 release](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/five-million-robots-now-operate-in-factories-globally) reports about five million industrial robots operating globally in 2025 and more than 600,000 installations that year. **Classification: trade-association deployment statistics.**
- [Its September 30 service-robot release](https://ifr.org/ifr-press-releases/news/global-sales-of-professional-service-robots-surge-24-percent) reports 117,500 transport/logistics units sold in 2025 and describes a much smaller full-size humanoid segment, with commercial and technical constraints. **Classification: market shipments and industry interpretation.**

## Structure

1. Define industrial stock, annual installations, service-robot sales, and humanoid shipments. Explain why these are not one common denominator.
2. Follow one generic material-handling workflow and identify what is automated versus what requires people. Label it as a conceptual example until a facility is reported.
3. Explain why structured environments can support narrower automation before general-purpose humanoids become economical.
4. Examine labor channels separately: changed tasks, maintenance, integration, throughput, staffing, and safety. Do not infer their magnitudes from robot counts.
5. Give readers a framework for judging a deployment claim: paid deployment, utilization, human interventions, total operating cost, and repeat orders.

## Counterargument and limits

Robotics is not synonymous with generative AI. Machines may use conventional controls, specialized perception, AI models, or combinations. Shipments do not show productive operating hours, a replacement ratio, or layoffs. A logistics robot and an industrial arm are not interchangeable worker equivalents. Trade-association forecasts and promotional framing require attribution.

## Reader value and visual

Build separate panels for installed industrial stock and annual category flows, with **2025** prominent. A conceptual deployment checklist is more defensible than a chart of “jobs replaced.” If adding a geography comparison, extract and verify all country figures from the same release and definitions.

## Remaining reporting and refresh

Find a named customer's deployment with published operating evidence and seek worker or technician input. Ask about intervention time and retained staffing, not only robot throughput. Review IFR source methods and reuse permissions before creating a numerical graphic. Source register: S15 and S16.
