# The AI coding productivity debate needs a reset

Status: unpublished research brief; editorial review pending. Research cutoff: November 5, 2026. Proposed format: 1,300–1,600-word software-industry explainer. Audience: developers, engineering managers, technology executives, and prospective software workers.

## The angle

Show why a model completing a coding benchmark, a developer feeling faster, and a team shipping useful software are different claims. The most compelling story is how researchers changed their approach as the work itself changed.

## Proposed opening

Ask whether AI makes developers faster and you may get a benchmark score, a personal estimate, or the time taken to close an issue. Each can be informative. None measures the entire journey from a business problem to software that works reliably for its users.

## Evidence to build on

- [METR's February 24 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) explains why its follow-up experiment became hard to interpret: developer and task selection, a compensation change, and concurrent agent use. It does not offer a clean universal 2026 speedup estimate. **Classification: research-design update.**
- [Its May 11 study](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/) reports a 349-person convenience survey, collected February–April. Respondents' perceived value gains differ from perceived speed gains; low response and selection limit generalization. **Classification: self-report evidence.**
- [Indeed's July vacancy analysis](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/) provides hiring context, not proof that coding agents caused the recovery. **Classification: platform demand signal.**

## Structure

1. Explain the distinction between writing code, completing a development task, and creating useful product value.
2. Describe what the original 2025 experiment measured, with its date and population attached. Explain why neither “AI slows developers” nor “that result no longer matters” is adequate.
3. Follow the redesign: the people and tasks willing to enter a randomized no-AI condition can become unrepresentative.
4. Examine the unit of work when one engineer supervises several agents. Elapsed time, human attention, computing cost, and review load diverge.
5. Propose an engineering scorecard: accepted changes, escaped defects, incident burden, review effort, delivery lead time, and business usefulness.

## Counterargument and limits

Measurement difficulty is not evidence that gains are imaginary, nor a license to declare huge gains unmeasurable and therefore unquestionable. A survey is useful for forming hypotheses but cannot replace a controlled outcome measure. Faster production of code is not a reliable staffing formula by itself.

## Reader value and visual

Use an evidence ladder: **benchmark capability → task performance → team delivery → business outcome → staffing decision**. Each step gets its own example and missing measurement. Offer a pilot plan comparing similar work while tracking quality and reviewer effort; do not prescribe a headcount target.

## Remaining reporting and refresh

Check for newer METR results before writing. Seek an engineering team's before-and-after data with a stable definition of accepted work. Explicitly label any anecdote as an anecdote. Source register: S07, S08, S06; cross-link article 2 for seniority rather than repeat its numbers.
