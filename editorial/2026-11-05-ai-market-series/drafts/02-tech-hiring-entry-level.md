# The tech hiring rebound has an entry-level problem

A company can be hiring software engineers and have little interest in hiring its first-year software engineers. For an applicant trying to enter the field, that difference matters more than a headline announcing a recovery.

It also changes how we should discuss AI and careers. The relevant question extends beyond whether software work survives. We need to ask how people gain the experience that employers want, especially if the tasks once assigned to beginners become easier to automate.

The 2026 evidence points to a recovery worth examining closely, with clear limits on what it can tell us about cause.

## Recovery for whom?

Indeed Hiring Lab's July analysis found that 71% of the increase in software-development postings between May 2025 and May 2026 came from senior roles. Its broader charts described a recovery from a depressed starting point, with software postings still below their pre-pandemic level. These are vacancy observations through the reported period, not a claim about all hiring today. [Indeed Hiring Lab](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/).

That finding makes the recovery more specific. It does not say 71% of all software jobs are senior jobs. It describes who contributed to an increase. Nor does a job posting guarantee a hire, a filled position, or an opportunity available in every location.

Industry labels can cause another misunderstanding. Challenger's November report explains that its technology-sector hiring plans include Amazon's seasonal warehouse and fulfillment roles. “Technology hiring” in that classification therefore cannot be read automatically as software-engineering recruitment. [Challenger, Gray & Christmas](https://www.challengergray.com/blog/october-layoffs-at-three-year-low-seasonal-hiring-plans-up-26/).

Someone choosing a career needs to inspect the actual role, not just the employer's sector or an aggregate arrow pointing upward.

## The payroll evidence adds a different view

Stanford's Canaries Dashboard, built with ADP Research, tracks employment in a balanced sample of ADP client firms. The page, displaying an October 21, 2026 update when rechecked, describes weaker trajectories for younger workers in highly exposed occupations, including software development, than for older workers in those roles. Exposure scores describe potential susceptibility to AI-related change; they do not verify each employer's adoption. [Stanford Digital Economy Lab](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/canaries-dashboard/).

Age and experience also differ. A 24-year-old with several years of relevant work and a 40-year-old changing careers do not fit neatly into “junior” and “senior” categories based on birth date. The dashboard helps identify a pattern. It cannot describe every beginner's prospects.

The hiring and payroll measures are useful together precisely because they observe different things. One shows what employers advertise. Another shows who is employed in a particular sample. Neither alone explains why a specific application went unanswered.

## AI is one possible driver in a crowded explanation

Stanford SIEPR's July research synthesis discusses interest rates, pandemic-era hiring, remote work, and the timing of declines as competing or interacting explanations. It treats AI's contribution to the difficult graduate market as an active empirical question. [Mahoney, McEntarfer, and Wahal](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/policy-brief/what-really-happening-jobs-separating-ai-hype-reality).

That uncertainty should shape the response. If an employer's budget is constrained, a hiring freeze may continue even where AI adds little. If tools change task costs, the employer may redesign a role. If both occur, a simple explanation can hide the contribution of each.

One plausible mechanism deserves particular scrutiny: employers may prefer experienced people who can direct tools and recognize problems in their output. That is a hypothesis about incentives, not an established explanation of the entire hiring pattern. It also creates a practical question for those employers. Where will the next group of experienced people come from?

## Experience needs a production process too

Imagine an engineering team that assigns small bug fixes, test maintenance, and documentation work to new hires. Those tasks serve two purposes: producing useful work and teaching how the system behaves. If a tool completes more of the production, the learning function still needs an owner.

This is a hypothetical team, but the management problem is concrete. A training plan can identify which decisions a learner should practice, what feedback they receive, and which responsibilities they can take on safely. Generating more code does not automatically provide that experience.

Employers could make this visible through paid apprenticeships, explicit review time, and assignments that require explaining tradeoffs. They could evaluate a new worker's ability to investigate a failure, interpret a requirement, and respond to feedback alongside their use of tools.

Such arrangements cost time. That cost should appear in the hiring plan. Expecting every candidate to arrive fully trained simply transfers it to workers and other employers.

## A more useful response for applicants

A portfolio can show more than a finished interface. It can record the original problem, the constraints, the tool assistance used, and the checks that caught mistakes. A short explanation of a failed approach may reveal more judgment than another polished demo.

Treat that as a way to make evidence legible, not a guarantee of an interview. Hiring decisions still depend on demand, geography, access, and employer practices. Workers should not be blamed for failing to overcome a market with too few genuine entry routes.

When evaluating a listing, inspect its supervision and learning arrangements as carefully as its technology requirements. Does “entry level” include training? Who reviews the work? Has the team recently hired beginners? What would competent performance look like after several months?

For employers, the corresponding question is whether the role genuinely permits learning. A team that wants experienced judgment needs a way to develop it. A recovery that excludes that investment may improve today's vacancy figures while leaving tomorrow's talent pipeline weaker.

## What to watch next

Watch whether subsequent posting gains broaden beyond senior roles, whether employers report actual junior hires rather than open requisitions, and whether payroll patterns change across age groups. Employer evidence on paid apprenticeships, supervision, and progression would help show whether the entry route itself is improving. Those are monitoring questions, not forecasts of an individual applicant’s outcome.
