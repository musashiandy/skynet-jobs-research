# “Just reskill” is not a career-transition plan

A chart can put declining occupations on one side and growing occupations on the other. The person expected to move between them still needs a route they can actually take.

That route has a timetable, a cost, an income requirement, and a location. It may involve a credential. It needs an employer willing to hire someone making the transition. A course can be part of it, but a course cannot supply all of those conditions.

This is the practical weakness in much advice about AI and careers. Telling people to adapt identifies a responsibility without explaining how they can fulfill it.

## New work and accessible work are different things

McKinsey Global Institute's September report models roughly 11 million U.S. workers potentially needing occupational transitions over the next decade, with a six-to-16-million scenario range. Its analysis includes AI, broader automation, demographics, and growth, and examines barriers involving skills, wages, credentials, and location. These are modeled pathways and labor-demand scenarios, not an observed count of people displaced by AI. [McKinsey Global Institute](https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/our-research/workforce-in-motion-skills-and-pathways-to-future-jobs-in-the-united-states).

The range is useful for thinking about preparation. It is unsuitable as a precise forecast of who will lose a job. It also cannot tell a particular worker whether an opening will exist near them when they complete training.

Even a growing field can be difficult to enter. [Indeed's July analysis](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/) describes a software vacancy recovery concentrated in senior roles. The related article on [entry-level technology hiring](02-tech-hiring-entry-level.md) explores what that distinction means for beginners. It belongs in every transition plan.

Someone moving into a different occupation may have substantial work experience and still be new to the destination role. Advice that considers only the number of advertised jobs can miss this entry barrier.

## Build a route around the actual constraints

Begin with a target role and a specific labor market. Read current listings, identify the tasks, and distinguish requirements that recur from preferences that appear occasionally. Check legally required qualifications with the relevant authority rather than assuming every employer preference is a legal barrier.

Then identify what the worker can already demonstrate. A familiar skill may need a different vocabulary or a different form of evidence in the destination field. Another requirement may represent a real gap that takes supervised practice to close.

The next question is how to cross that gap while remaining financially viable. Training fees are only part of the cost. Reduced working hours, travel, equipment, care responsibilities, and uncertainty during a search can determine whether the plan is possible.

These are planning principles, not a recommendation for a particular occupation or credential. Their purpose is to replace an abstract destination with a sequence of decisions that can be checked.

## A pathway scorecard

Before committing significant time or money, a worker and any supporting organization could complete a scorecard like this one. Unknowns should remain visible rather than being converted into optimistic assumptions.

| Question | Evidence to collect |
| --- | --- |
| Is there accessible demand? | Current local or genuinely available remote vacancies, including entry requirements |
| What experience transfers? | Examples of relevant work, assessed against destination tasks |
| What must be learned? | Specific gaps and a way to demonstrate competence |
| What qualification is necessary? | Verified legal requirements, separated from employer preferences |
| Can the transition be financed? | Full costs, income during training, and a realistic search allowance |
| Who provides experience? | Paid placements, supervised work, or an employer-backed entry route |
| How will the plan be revised? | Review dates, stop conditions, and a fallback if demand changes |

The table deliberately avoids a single “future-proof” score. A strength in one column cannot pay for a critical constraint in another. Excellent transferable skills do not remove an unavailable placement or an income gap a household cannot absorb.

## Employers can change the route

Employers influence several of these barriers. They can make requirements clearer, offer paid learning time, recognize relevant experience, and create supervised entry roles. They can also identify which qualifications are necessary and which have simply become habitual filters.

These are proposed choices, not interventions proven by the scenario report. Their attraction is that they address the hiring process directly. A training initiative with no connection to accessible work leaves the worker to solve the remaining problems alone.

An organization redesigning roles around AI could start with its own staff. Map which responsibilities are changing and which capabilities will be needed. Then compare those needs with the people already employed, including the time and supervision required for a credible move.

Redeployment will not always be feasible. The point is to evaluate it concretely, rather than invoking reskilling as a reassuring phrase after the staffing decision has already been made.

## Ask a training program for outcomes

A useful program should be able to explain who enrolls, who completes, who enters relevant work, and what happens to pay and retention. Definitions matter. A placement statistic could count many kinds of work; a completion statistic says little about the opportunity afterward.

Readers can ask how outcomes differ by prior experience and whether the program has evidence from people with similar starting conditions. They can ask what support exists if the first pathway fails. Strong answers are more useful than a catalog of rapidly changing tools.

No program can guarantee the future labor market. It can still be clear about its evidence, its obligations, and the part of the transition it actually supports.

AI-related change should make practical career planning more important, not make workers accept less specific advice. A serious transition plan ends with a route into work that can sustain a life, and names who will help make that route possible.
