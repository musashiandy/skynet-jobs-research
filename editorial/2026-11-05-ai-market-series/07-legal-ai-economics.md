# AI can write the brief. Who gets the savings?

Status: unpublished research brief; editorial review pending. Research cutoff: November 5, 2026. Proposed format: 1,200–1,500-word legal-services economics feature. Audience: legal professionals, professional-services workers, business clients, and managers of expertise-based teams.

## The angle

Explore how the same time-saving tool can affect price, billable capacity, service quality, and staffing differently. Treat the distribution of gains as the story. The headline refers to drafting assistance, not a finding that AI can autonomously produce reliable, court-ready work.

## Proposed opening

If research that once took hours can be drafted much faster, who should benefit? A client might expect a smaller bill. A firm might expect more capacity. A junior lawyer might need a new way to learn. Legal AI makes an old business question newly urgent: what exactly is the customer paying for?

## Evidence to build on

- [Clio's October 26 report announcement](https://www.clio.com/about/press/2026-legal-trends-report/) combines surveys and operating data. It reports higher revenue and billable hours among some AI-tool adopters, and limited respondent-reported AI-related staffing reductions. Its 42% billable-workday figure is a **projection**, not a completed full-year outcome. **Classification: vendor research, associations, surveys, and forecast.**
- [Thomson Reuters' 2026 legal report](https://www.thomsonreuters.com/en/institute/future-of-professionals-2026/report-legal) draws on March–April surveys, including 736 law-firm respondents and 203 corporate legal responses. It captures client pressure and business-model choices. **Classification: vendor-sponsored survey and interpretation.**

## Structure

1. Start with an explicitly hypothetical fixed legal assignment and identify production time, review time, fees, and responsibility.
2. Compare the economics of hourly billing, a fixed fee, and expanded service capacity. Do not imply any is universally preferable or ethically sufficient.
3. Explain why adopter revenue differences cannot prove that purchasing a product caused those differences.
4. Examine training and career progression as management choices: how will junior professionals develop judgment when first drafts become cheaper?
5. Ask whether clients receive demonstrable quality or cost benefits, not just assurances that AI is being used.

## Counterargument and limits

Both source organizations sell into the legal market. Different samples, definitions, and field periods prevent combining their percentages. No widespread headcount reductions in a surveyed sample does not guarantee future protection. Nor does lower drafting time establish reliable citations, better advice, or lower client fees.

## Reader value and visual

Offer an original hypothetical fee-and-capacity model with the assumptions visible. Vary production effort, verification effort, price, and volume separately. Keep invented dollar amounts labeled as illustrative. A reader should leave able to ask where a claimed gain appears in service outcomes, rather than infer value from tool adoption.

## Remaining reporting and refresh

Review the full Clio report's methodology and sampling before using detailed numbers in final copy. Seek a client's actual matter-level comparison and a firm's documented training approach. Check applicable professional guidance only if the finished article makes specific ethics claims. Source register: S12 and S13.
