# “Just reskill” is not a career-transition plan

Status: unpublished research brief; editorial review pending. Research cutoff: November 5, 2026. Proposed format: 1,300–1,600-word career-transition and workforce-policy feature. Audience: career changers, displaced workers, employers, educators, and workforce-development teams.

## The angle

Turn the abstract promise of new jobs into a practical question: can the worker who needs a transition actually enter one? Skills overlap is only part of feasibility. Training time, legal credentials, hiring preferences, geography, wages, and time without income can determine whether a pathway exists in practice.

## Proposed opening

A forecast can put declining occupations on one side of a chart and growing occupations on the other. It cannot pay the rent during training or make a distant vacancy local. For someone changing careers, the line between those columns needs a budget, a timetable, and an employer willing to hire.

## Evidence to build on

[McKinsey Global Institute's September 29 report](https://www.mckinsey.com/mgi/our-research/workforce-in-motion-skills-and-pathways-to-future-jobs-in-the-united-states) models roughly 11 million workers potentially needing occupational transitions, with a six-to-16-million scenario range over the next decade. It analyzes skill, wage, credential, and location barriers under AI, broader automation, demographic, and growth assumptions. **Classification: scenario modeling, not completed displacement.**

[Indeed's July analysis](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/) illustrates why recovery in a field's vacancies does not establish an accessible entry route. **Classification: observed platform demand, not a test of a training program.**

## Structure

1. Explain the difference between net job growth, gross changes in labor demand, and actual workers who must move.
2. Unpack what “adjacent skills” leaves out. Distinguish a legally required license from an employer preference.
3. Construct a clearly hypothetical transition plan with prerequisites, training hours, total cost, forgone earnings, local demand checks, and a fallback.
4. Compare responsibility: what can a worker do, what should an employer fund, and where are education or policy changes needed?
5. Close with evidence a program should publish: enrollment, completion, placement, retention, wage change, and outcomes for comparable nonparticipants.

## Counterargument and limits

The existence of barriers does not mean training is futile. It means course completion alone is an inadequate outcome. The MGI range incorporates several structural forces and cannot be described as “AI will eliminate 11 million jobs.” A projected growing occupation is not a guaranteed vacancy or a personalized recommendation to buy a credential.

## Reader value and visual

Create an original pathway scorecard with separate fields for skills, credentials, location, time, income, and verified hiring demand. Keep unknowns visible. Avoid a single “future-proof” score. Show a worked example only after current local requirements and costs are verified; until then, label all illustrative inputs as hypothetical.

## Remaining reporting and refresh

Choose one real transition corridor and verify local vacancies, credential requirements, and training costs from original sources before drafting a case study. Interview a workforce program about placement definitions and wage outcomes. This proposal makes no paid-course recommendation. Source register: S20 and S06.
