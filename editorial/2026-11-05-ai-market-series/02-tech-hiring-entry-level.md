# The tech hiring rebound has an entry-level problem

Status: unpublished research brief; editorial review pending. Research cutoff: November 5, 2026. Proposed format: 1,200–1,500-word career and labor-market analysis. Audience: students, recent graduates, hiring managers, and workers moving into software.

## The angle

A recovery in vacancies does not automatically reopen the first rung of a career. Examine the composition of the recovery and the difference between available jobs, completed hires, and people on payroll. This updates the site's earlier career-ladder argument with specific evidence rather than repeating a prediction.

## Proposed opening

“Tech jobs are coming back” sounds like good news until you ask which jobs. A vacancy requiring years of production experience may improve a hiring chart without helping someone searching for their first role. To understand the recovery, the job title is only the start of the story.

## Evidence to build on

- [Indeed's July 8 analysis](https://hiringlab.indeed.com/2026/07/08/ai-and-job-postings-from-destruction-to-creation/) attributes 71% of the increase in software-development postings from May 2025 to May 2026 to senior roles. It also says the recovery starts from a depressed level. **Classification: observed platform vacancies.**
- [Stanford and ADP's dashboard](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/project/indicators/canaries-dashboard/) shows differing employment trajectories by age and exposure, including early-career software and customer-service workers. **Classification: payroll-sample trends grouped by modeled exposure.**
- [Stanford SIEPR's July synthesis](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/policy-brief/what-really-happening-jobs-separating-ai-hype-reality) considers monetary tightening, pandemic hiring, remote work, and timing as alternative or interacting explanations. **Classification: research interpretation.**

## Structure

1. Separate the recovery's size from its composition. Explain the denominator of “share of the increase.”
2. Put a vacancy measure beside a payroll measure without treating them as interchangeable.
3. Explain why “ages 22–25,” “junior,” “new graduate,” and “newly hired” identify different groups.
4. Explore a management hypothesis: employers may want experienced people to supervise agent output while reducing routine learning assignments. Label this as a hypothesis, not an established explanation of the charts.
5. Show what a credible entry route could include: supervised production experience, debugging and review, a portfolio with documented decisions, and paid apprenticeships. Present these as proposals to test, not promises of employment.

## Counterargument and limits

The market also reflects interest rates, budgets, outsourcing, and earlier over-hiring. AI exposure is not proof of an employer's actual use. A stronger senior hiring market does not establish that junior talent is unnecessary. Refresh the dashboard before using any numerical endpoint; the packet intentionally asserts no new dashboard percentage.

## Reader value and visual

Provide a checklist for evaluating an “entry-level” listing: experience requirement, supervision, training allocation, evidence of an actual junior cohort, and responsibilities after onboarding. Build a labeled small-multiple visual showing vacancies and payroll separately, with distinct periods and units. Never add the overlapping seniority and AI-title contributions.

Request current posting breakdowns and ask hiring managers how they are funding apprenticeship. Include a junior worker's experience only with actual reporting and permission. Verify source periods again before drafting. Source register: S04–S06; S03 is a useful firm-level counterpoint.
